Railside Robotics · It Passed Inspection

An invertible robot built around a drum weapon

Our three-person team designed and competed a Plastic Ant Weight robot through Railside Robotics. I helped develop the chassis, drum, and front forks from early sketches into a refined CAD assembly and physical prototype.

Role
Mechanical design: chassis, drum, and front forks
Class
PlAnt (Plastic Ant Weight), up to 1 lb
Build
About 4.5 × 5.2 in; PLA body and weapon, foam wheels
Weapon
2822 1800KV motor; estimated peak tip speed around 250 mph

01

Designing for either orientation

The robot was designed to keep driving even after it flipped over.

At our first meeting, we explored possible layouts and formed our three-person team. Wheel placement let the robot drive upside down, while the drum weapon gave the design its central mechanical challenge.

The PlAnt class caps the robot at one pound and requires its structural and weapon parts to be plastic. We used PLA for those printed components, with foam wheels coated in liquid latex. Two drive motors controlled the wheels through a single gimbal on our RadioMaster Pocket transmitter.

02

Early concepts

Planning the layout

The team moved from broad shape exploration to a layout sketch that called out the major components. It proposed a central drum with two smaller drums at the sides, alongside the battery, wheels, and electronics.

I missed this planning meeting, so my teammates created the initial layout. It gave us a starting point as I joined the chassis, drum, and fork design work in CAD.

03

Mechanical design

From the first model to a full assembly

During the third meeting, we started the first Onshape iteration. I helped design the chassis, drum, and front forks, while the team developed the frame and wheel layout together.

I mounted the drum to its planned motor and mated the assembly to the frame. Checking that it could rotate in the model gave us an early integration check before moving to the physical build.

In later CAD iterations, we refined the chassis shape and fit, made room for the electronics, and reduced the footprint while building up the printed walls.

For the drum, we used a 2822 1800KV motor. A club calculator estimated a peak tip speed of about 250 mph, a speed the weapon could reasonably reach in a match.

04

Competition test

A direct hit exposed the weak point

After the second match at the first onboarding competition, an opponent hit the center of the drum head-on and split it in half. The robot's weapon became its weak point, even though the chassis withstood repeated hits and stayed in service across multiple matches.

The team did not have an on-site 3D printer, so we could not replace or revise the drum during that competition. In one match, we came close to a win through control points, but the weapon failure continued to limit the robot.

05

Competition results and the next iteration

The first competition showed that the chassis could take repeated impacts, but the drum needed to survive direct hits.

We lost our matches after the drum failed. For the second onboarding competition, we requested a stronger replacement drum, but long print queues and no on-site printer meant it was not ready in time. We competed with the remaining copies of the original design. The chassis and plates held up again, but another direct hit split the drum. Across both competitions, the chassis proved durable while the weapon remained the limiting component.

Our working explanation was that too few outer walls and weak infill let the drum fracture. The planned reprint would increase the wall count to 15 and reduce infill from 15% to below 10%, with a different pattern such as honeycomb. Since that revision was not printed in time, we did not get to test whether it would solve the problem.
